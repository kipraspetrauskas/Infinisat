INFINISAT - THE IDEA AND POINT OF THE GAME
==========================================
Last updated: 2026-11-14
Engine: Unreal Engine 5.8 (installed at C:\Program Files\Epic Games\UE_5.8)
Budget: zero. Free tools, free and open-licence assets only.


1. THE CHALLENGE (NASA Space Apps 2026: "Space Mission Design Game")
--------------------------------------------------------------------
Designing a space mission means balancing demands that compete with each other:
mission objectives, spacecraft design, scientific instruments, launch vehicles,
budget, power, mass, communications and orbital constraints. Students rarely get
to try a whole mission from concept to operation, so these trade-offs stay
abstract.

The challenge: build an interactive game where players design, manage and
simulate a complete space mission. They make engineering decisions, manage
limited resources, and see how each choice shapes whether the mission succeeds.

What Infinisat must show to answer the challenge:
  - Every design choice costs something: mass, power, money, data rate or time.
  - The full mission life cycle: request -> design -> build -> launch -> orbit
    -> operations -> end of life (deorbit or graveyard orbit).
  - Clear feedback that explains WHY a mission succeeded or failed.
  - Real-world grounding: the numbers come from real missions (see section 4).


2. ONE-LINE PITCH
-----------------
You are BIT, a small spherical engineering robot. Clients send you orders. You
design, build, launch and fly satellites into a crowded, realistic sky, starting
with a CanSat from your back yard and ending with a probe that leaves the solar
system.


3. WHAT MAKES INFINISAT DIFFERENT
---------------------------------
Infinisat is about the SATELLITE, not the rocket. Rockets are mostly bought as a
launch service (pick vehicle, launch site, rideshare or dedicated). The depth goes
into the spacecraft itself:

  - Bus design at subsystem level: structure, power (solar, battery, RTG),
    thermal, attitude control (magnetorquers, reaction wheels, star trackers,
    thrusters), propulsion, onboard computer, communications, payload.
  - Budgets that must close: mass budget, power budget (sunlight vs eclipse),
    link budget (antenna size, frequency band, distance -> data rate),
    thermal range, delta-v budget, money and schedule.
  - Failure modes taken from real history (e.g. Landsat 1's camera power fault,
    section 4). Parts have reliability; redundancy costs mass but saves missions.
  - A shared, crowded sky: thousands of existing satellites (GNSS, weather,
    Starlink-style constellations). Players can RENT existing networks (ground
    stations, relay satellites, GNSS for navigation) instead of building their
    own, and must avoid collisions and debris.
  - Scale from tiny to huge: CanSat (350 g, balloon or hobby rocket) -> CubeSat
    -> small sat -> GEO comsat -> lunar orbiter -> RTG-powered outer planet probe
    -> solar-sail interstellar probe.

Legal / originality note: Kerbal Space Program is used ONLY as a design
reference (play it to study what feels good). Its code, models, textures,
sounds and names are not copied, decompiled or extracted. KSP is a Unity game
and its assets and code are copyrighted, and its EULA forbids reverse
engineering. Infinisat is built from scratch in Unreal with its own art style,
its own characters and a satellite-first design.


4. REAL-WORLD DATA (from the reference links)
---------------------------------------------
Use these numbers for in-game parts, orbits and missions.

4.1 Landsat 1 (sources: science.nasa.gov/mission/landsat-1, usgs.gov)
  - Launched 23 July 1972 as ERTS-1 on a Delta 900 from Vandenberg AFB.
  - Orbit: 917 km, sun-synchronous near-polar, inclination 99.2 deg,
    period 103 min (~14 orbits/day), repeat cycle 18 days.
    Equator crossing ~9:30-9:42 local time (descending).
  - Mass ~953 kg. 3 m tall, 1.5 m diameter, solar paddles span 4 m.
    Attitude control +/-0.7 deg. Hydrazine, 3 thrusters. S-band and VHF radio.
    Bus was a modified Nimbus weather satellite.
  - Instruments:
      RBV: 3 cameras, 80 m. A power-switching fault caused an attitude upset
           on 6 Aug 1972; the RBV was switched off 15 days after launch.
      MSS: 4 bands (green 0.5-0.6, red 0.6-0.7, NIR 0.7-0.8, NIR 0.8-1.1 um),
           80 m resolution, 185 km swath, 6-bit data. Became the main sensor.
  - Design life 1 year; actually ran until 6 Jan 1978 (~5.5 years).
  - First image: Dallas-Fort Worth, 25 July 1972. Imaged an 81,000-acre
    Alaska fire within days. Near-global land coverage by end of 1973.
  -> GAME USE: Chapter 2 "Eyes on Earth" mission template. Teaches
     sun-synchronous orbits, swath vs revisit time, instrument bands, and
     "design life vs actual life". The RBV fault becomes a scripted failure
     event the player must recover from.

4.2 GNSS (source: earthdata.nasa.gov/.../gnss)
  - GPS: 24 operational satellites, circular 12-hour orbits at 20,200 km.
    About 6 visible from anywhere at almost any time.
  - GLONASS: 24 operational (26 total), 19,140 km.
  - Galileo: operational since Dec 2016; 30 satellites (27 + 3 spares) in
    3 planes at 23,222 km.
  - BeiDou: 35 nominal: 5 GEO, 3 IGSO, 27 MEO.
  - Regional: QZSS (Japan, 4 sats), NavIC (India, geosynchronous).
  - Receivers decode ranging codes on two radio carriers. Geodesy uses
    several hundred permanent ground receivers to measure plate motion,
    earthquake displacement and Earth orientation. Many satellites also
    carry laser retroreflectors.
  -> GAME USE: Chapter 1 "Where Am I?" (build one GNSS satellite and fill a gap
     in a constellation; learn MEO, clocks, coverage, why you need 4+ satellites
     for a position fix). Later, GNSS is a SERVICE the player's own satellites
     use for navigation in LEO.

4.3 Starlink / mega-constellations (source: planet4589.org, data 13 Nov 2026)
  - 13,096 launched; 20 failed to reach orbit; 1,903 re-entered.
  - 11,193 in orbit, 11,179 working, 9,704 in operational orbit.
  - Shells at inclinations 43, 53, 70 and 97 deg.
  - Gen3: 46 launched, 20 failed to orbit, none operational yet.
  -> GAME USE: the sky is crowded. Starlink-style shells appear as a live
     background population (generic name in game, e.g. "MegaNet"). Collision
     avoidance, debris and end-of-life disposal are real mechanics. A
     late-game order asks for a deorbit-compliant constellation.

4.4 Weather satellites (rmets.org page blocked automated access; the figures
    below are standard reference values, check them against the article)
  - Geostationary (GEO): 35,786 km above the equator, period = 1 day, stays
    over one spot. Images a full disc every 10-15 min. Examples: Meteosat
    (EUMETSAT), GOES (NOAA), Himawari (Japan).
  - Polar-orbiting (LEO, ~800-850 km, sun-synchronous): covers the whole Earth
    twice a day in strips, higher resolution, sounders measure temperature and
    humidity profiles. Examples: Metop, NOAA-20/21.
  - Satellite data is the largest single input to numerical weather prediction.
  -> GAME USE: Chapter 3 "Storm Watch": the player chooses GEO (constant view,
     expensive launch, low resolution) vs polar (cheap, detailed, gaps in time)
     or both. A clear trade-off lesson.

5. PLAYER CHARACTER: "BIT"  (from Character.jpg / Character more detail.jpg)
----------------------------------------------------------------------------
  - Spherical robot body. Horizontal band around the middle.
  - One big circular eye in the centre (iris ring + dark pupil). The eye is the
    main way it shows emotion: widen, squint, blink, change colour.
  - Floating, detached hands (no arms): round palms with small floating
    fingertip spheres. Hands drift and bob; they snap together to build.
  - Small panel lines on the body (four short slashes).
  - Role: the hands do the building in the assembly hall (pick up parts, bolt,
    solder). The eye is the camera in tutorials. BIT can float around the
    satellite in the build bay.
  - Working name "BIT" can be changed.

7. ART STYLE
------------
"Not hyper-real, not PS2." Target: stylized, clean, readable.
  - Soft PBR materials with slightly bold colours. Clean silhouettes.
    Subtle outline post-process on parts in the build bay.
  - Earth: real NASA Blue Marble / Black Marble textures (public domain),
    slightly stylized atmosphere glow.
  - UI: blueprint / engineering-drawing look (white lines on navy) for design
    screens, so budgets read like real spec sheets.
  - Satellites: chunky readable parts, gold MLI foil, blue solar cells. They
    should look like real hardware, just simplified.


8. GAME STRUCTURE
-----------------
8.1 Core loop
  ORDER BOARD -> DESIGN (bus + payload, budgets must close) -> TEST (thermal-vac,
  vibration, cost/time vs risk) -> LAUNCH (buy a ride: vehicle, site, slot)
  -> COMMISSIONING (deploy panels, detumble, first contact) -> OPERATIONS
  (time-warp, downlink data, handle anomalies) -> RESULTS (client score,
  money, reputation, unlocks) -> END OF LIFE (deorbit, graveyard, or keep).

8.2 Resources the player manages
  Money, reputation, schedule (deadlines), research points, ground station
  time, launch slots, and the in-orbit budgets: mass, power, data, delta-v,
  thermal.

8.3 Campaign (progression from basic to interstellar)
  Ch 0  Back Yard     CanSat on a weather balloon / hobby rocket. Basics:
                      mass, battery, radio range, parachute.
  Ch 1  Where Am I?   GNSS satellite at MEO (20,200 km). Clocks, coverage,
                      constellation geometry.
  Ch 2  Eyes on Earth Landsat-like imager, sun-synchronous 900 km.
                      Resolution vs swath vs data rate.
  Ch 3  Storm Watch   Weather: GEO vs polar trade.
  Ch 4  Connect       Comms constellation in a crowded LEO. Debris and
                      deorbit rules. Use or compete with MegaNet.
  Ch 5  Moonshot      Lunar orbiter that maps and ranks landing sites
                      (slope, sunlight, comms visibility to Earth, ice).
  Ch 6  Big Planet    RTG ("nuclear") probe to Jupiter; radiation hardening,
                      Deep Space Network-style rented antennas, light-time
                      delay. Final act: atmospheric entry probe that "beeps"
                      until it is crushed.
  Ch 7  Infinity      Solar-sail interstellar probe (the logo mission). Leave
                      the solar system; data return across decades.
  Sandbox: free design of anything from a CanSat to an interstellar probe.

8.4 EDUCATION IS THE CORE (every mechanic must teach something)
  Rule: no number in the game is magic. Every value can be clicked to show
  WHERE it comes from (formula + real-world example + source).

  a) Learning objectives per chapter, shown before and checked after:
     Ch0 mass, battery capacity, radio range, drag/parachute
     Ch1 orbital period (Kepler's 3rd law), MEO, atomic clocks, trilateration,
         why 4 satellites for a GNSS fix
     Ch2 sun-synchronous orbit, swath, resolution, revisit, spectral bands,
         data volume vs downlink
     Ch3 GEO vs LEO trade, coverage, latency, launch cost per kg
     Ch4 constellations, collision risk, debris, deorbit rules
     Ch5 transfer orbits, delta-v (rocket equation), lunar landing-site
         criteria, light-time
     Ch6 inverse-square sunlight -> RTG, radiation, Deep Space Network,
         link budget over huge distances
     Ch7 solar sail physics (radiation pressure), escape velocity,
         heliopause, decades-long missions
  b) BIT is the mentor: short hints, "Why?" buttons, and a post-mission
     debrief that names the ONE limiting factor and how to fix it
     ("Your battery emptied in eclipse at minute 36").
  c) Live formulas in the design screen, e.g. T = 2*pi*sqrt(a^3/mu),
     dv = Isp*g0*ln(m0/mf), battery depth = load * eclipse_time / capacity.
     Values update as parts change, so cause and effect is visible.
  d) "Compare with reality": after each mission the player's design is shown
     next to the real one (Landsat 1, GOES, LRO, Juno, Voyager...) with
     mass, power, lifetime side by side, using the NASA 3D models.
  e) Quick checks: 2-3 multiple-choice questions per chapter (unlock bonus
     parts). "Sat-o-pedia" encyclopedia of every part and real spacecraft.
  f) Failure is a lesson: anomalies come from real history (Landsat 1 RBV
     power fault) and the debrief explains what engineers did.
  g) Teacher/judge mode: all chapters unlocked, sandbox, exportable report
     of choices and results.


9. TECHNICAL PLAN (Unreal Engine 5.8, free tools only)
------------------------------------------------------
  - Physics: do NOT simulate orbits with the physics engine. Use patched-conic
    two-body orbits (Kepler) computed in C++, with double precision and
    floating origin (UE5 Large World Coordinates). Time warp is analytic.
  - Background satellites: load real orbital elements (TLEs, free from
    CelesTrak) and propagate with SGP4 (open-source C++ implementation by
    Vallado, free licence). Render thousands as instanced points (Niagara).
  - Data-driven parts: every part is a row in a DataTable/JSON (mass, power,
    cost, reliability, size), so designers add parts without code.
  - Systems: Mission/Contract system, Design validator (budgets), Ops simulator
    (power/thermal/data tick per orbit), Event/anomaly system, Save/load.
  - UI: UMG (+ CommonUI plugin, built in).
  - Two PCs (local + AnyDesk machine): use Git + Git LFS (free on GitHub up to
    the free limits) or a free Perforce Helix Core server (free for up to
    5 users) for version control. Do not sync a UE project via OneDrive; it
    corrupts files. Move the project out of OneDrive, e.g. C:\Dev\Infinisat.

  Free asset sources (check each licence; CC0 or public domain preferred):
    - NASA 3D Resources (nasa3d.arc.nasa.gov / science.nasa.gov 3D models):
      public domain spacecraft models, NASA image use guidelines apply
      (no NASA logo as endorsement).
    - NASA Visible Earth: Blue Marble / Black Marble Earth textures.
    - Solar System Scope textures (CC BY 4.0) for planets.
    - Quixel Megascans / Fab free content (free for Unreal use).
    - Poly Haven (CC0) HDRIs, textures, models.
    - Kenney.nl (CC0) UI and sound.
    - Blender (modelling), Krita/Inkscape/GIMP (2D), Audacity (sound),
      freesound.org (check per-file licence).
